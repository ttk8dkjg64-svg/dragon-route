How to Pay in China as a Foreigner (2026 Guide)

China is one of the world's most cashless societies — but the system was built for locals with Chinese bank accounts. Here's everything you need to set up before you land, what the 2026 rules changed, and how to pay without friction from day one.

From street dumplings to high-speed train tickets — one QR code scan pays for almost everything in China.

How China Actually Pays

In Chinese cities, most people haven't carried a wallet in years. Payment is a QR code scan — at restaurants, convenience stores, wet markets, taxis, tourist attractions, and street-food carts. Alipay and WeChat Pay together handle over 90% of all retail transactions. In Shanghai and Shenzhen the figure is even higher.

The good news: since 2023 both apps accept foreign Visa and Mastercard. The catch: you must link your card before you arrive. Setup inside China is significantly harder due to internet restrictions.

Dominant apps
Alipay + WeChat Pay
Foreign cards
Visa, Mastercard, Amex, JCB
Currency
Chinese Yuan (CNY / RMB / ¥)
Transaction fee
~3% on amounts over ¥200

What Changed in 2026

Two key updates. First, from February 1, 2026, China enforces strict penalties for merchants who refuse physical cash (RMB) — cash is now legally protected. If a vendor hesitates to accept banknotes, a polite reminder resolves it. Second, Alipay now supports 16 languages and the single-payment limit for foreigners rose from $1,000 to $5,000 USD.

Step-by-Step: Set Up Alipay International

  1. Download Alipay from the App Store or Google Play. Select English. Same app as the domestic version — look for International in payment settings.

  2. Create an account with your foreign phone number. Complete real-name verification using your passport photo.

  3. Add your Visa or Mastercard under "Me → Payment → Bank Cards." Credit cards work more reliably than debit cards.

  4. Test with a small transaction before flying. Call your bank ahead to whitelist Alipay — many banks block the first international payment.

  5. Enable Metro/Transit QR if visiting Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, or Chengdu — tap "Transport" in Alipay to pay for metro rides without a physical card.

Setup Timeline

Start 1–2 weeks before departure. Passport verification takes 24–48 hours, and bank approval may need a phone call. Don't leave this to the day you fly.

WeChat Pay — Essential Backup

WeChat Pay is built into WeChat — and WeChat is how China communicates. Your hotel, guide, restaurant reservations, and local contacts all use WeChat. Setup: download WeChat → "Me → Services → Pay → Wallet → Bank Cards" → link foreign Visa or Mastercard.

One thing many miss: WeChat requires verification by an existing WeChat user during signup. Ask a Chinese-speaking friend, or follow the on-screen WeChat Pay verification prompts. Set this up at home, not at the airport.

The 3% Fee — When It Applies

Using a foreign card through Alipay or WeChat Pay carries a 3% fee on single transactions above ¥200 (~$28). For daily purchases — street food, coffee, metro fares — there's no charge. For hotel bills or tour payments made through the apps, use a direct credit card or arrange hotel billing separately.

Cash: Still Useful, Now Legally Protected

Carry ¥500–1000 CNY cash as backup. Get it from your home bank (better rates) or from ICBC, Bank of China, or Agricultural Bank ATMs in China — these reliably accept foreign cards. Since February 2026, merchants must accept cash, so your banknotes work everywhere.

Hotel Deposits

Major international hotels, airports, and high-end restaurants accept Visa/Mastercard directly. Small restaurants and street stalls use Alipay/WeChat only. Hotels also charge a refundable cash deposit of ¥100–500 per night — plan your cash accordingly.

Pre-Departure Checklist

  • ✓ Alipay International — downloaded, passport verified, card linked
  • ✓ WeChat — account verified, backup card linked
  • ✓ Bank notified of China travel dates to prevent blocks
  • ✓ ¥500–1000 CNY cash obtained before flying
  • ✓ Debit card PIN memorized (ATMs require it)
  • ✓ No-foreign-fee travel card considered for large payments

China High-Speed Rail Guide for Foreign Tourists

China's bullet train network is the world's largest — 45,000+ km connecting every major city at up to 350 km/h. For most tourists it's faster than flying, cheaper than renting a car, and dramatically more comfortable than a bus.

The CR400 Fuxing series travels at 350 km/h — Beijing to Shanghai in 4.5 hours.

Why Take the Train?

Factor in check-in times, airport transfers, and security queues and high-speed rail beats flying on almost every route under 1,000 km. Beijing to Shanghai: 4.5 hours by G-train vs. 2-hour flight + 3 hours airport processing. The train drops you at a central city station, not 45 minutes outside it. Seats are wide, carriages quiet, there's a dining car, and power outlets at every seat.

Top speed
350 km/h (G-trains)
Network
45,000+ km nationwide
Tickets open
30 days in advance
Best booking site
Trip.com (English)

Train Types

  • G-trains (高铁) — Flagship high-speed, up to 350 km/h. Major routes: Beijing–Shanghai, Beijing–Xi'an, Shanghai–Chengdu. Use for any journey over 3 hours.
  • D-trains — Intercity express, 200–250 km/h. Good for shorter regional routes.
  • K/Z/T-trains — Conventional trains. Slower but essential for overnight sleeper routes to Tibet, Yunnan, and Xinjiang where high-speed rail doesn't yet reach.

How to Book Tickets

Trip.com — Best for Foreigners

Trip.com accepts foreign credit cards, has full English support, and delivers e-tickets to your email as a QR code. Small service fee (~¥25–50) — worth it. Use this unless you have a local Chinese phone number.

12306.cn — Official, No Fees

China's official rail site has an English interface and no service fees but requires SMS verification with a Chinese number. Works with a local SIM or eSIM.

Book Early

Tickets open exactly 30 days in advance at midnight Beijing time. For Golden Week (Oct 1–7), Spring Festival, and May Day: set an alarm and book the moment they open. Popular routes sell out within hours.

Seat Classes

  • Second Class (二等座) — 3+2 seats, air-conditioned, power outlets. Comfortable for any journey under 5 hours. Best value.
  • First Class (一等座) — 2+2 seats, more legroom. Worth the upgrade for 5+ hour journeys.
  • Business Class (商务座) — Lie-flat seats on some G-trains, meal service. Premium price, premium experience.
  • Hard Sleeper (硬卧) — Overnight conventional trains. 6 open bunks per bay, cheapest overnight option.
  • Soft Sleeper (软卧) — 4 bunks in a lockable compartment. More private, recommended for overnight routes.

Navigating Stations

Chinese train stations are large — the size of airports. Arrive at least 30–45 minutes before departure. All passengers pass through airport-style security. Show your e-ticket QR code at the gate. Board when your gate opens (~10–15 minutes before departure) — your car number (车厢号) is printed on the ticket.

Multiple Stations Warning

Many cities have multiple train stations. Beijing has South (南站), West (西站), and North (北站). Shanghai has Hongqiao (虹桥) and Shanghai Station. Always confirm which station your specific train uses when booking — and factor in transit time to get there.

China Visa Guide for Tourists 2026

China's entry requirements have shifted significantly since 2023. Many countries now have visa-free access, the 144-hour transit exemption has expanded, and the L tourist visa process is more streamlined. Here's what you need to know before you book anything.

Always verify current requirements with your country's Chinese embassy — policies change without notice.

Does Your Country Need a Visa?

As of 2026, citizens of most EU countries, the UK, Switzerland, Norway, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Japan, South Korea, and several other nations can enter China visa-free for tourism, typically 15–30 days depending on the bilateral agreement. The US, India, and many other nationalities still require a full visa.

Always verify your specific country's current status at the official Chinese Visa Application Service Center or your country's Chinese embassy — this is the only authoritative source.

Verify Before You Book

Visa policies change without notice. Any travel guide — including this one — may be outdated. Always confirm current requirements with the official Chinese embassy before purchasing non-refundable flights.

The 144-Hour Visa-Free Transit

Even if your country requires a visa, China's 144-Hour (6-day) Transit Visa-Free Policy lets eligible nationalities enter without a visa when genuinely transiting to a third country. Key conditions:

  • Confirmed onward ticket to a third country (not back to your departure country)
  • Entry and exit through designated cities — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Xi'an, and others
  • Must stay within the designated transit city's area
  • Passport valid for at least 6 months beyond entry date
  • No previous denial of a Chinese visa
Transit Strategy

Ideal for combining China with other Asia destinations. Fly into Beijing → explore for 5 days → fly onward to Tokyo or Bangkok. No visa needed for many nationalities. Confirm eligibility at your Chinese embassy before booking.

The L Tourist Visa — Documents

If your country requires a full visa, the standard tourist category is the L Visa, processed through the Chinese Visa Application Service Center (CVASC) or directly at a Chinese consulate.

  • Valid passport — at least 6 months validity beyond travel dates, minimum 2 blank pages
  • Completed visa application form (from visaforchina.cn)
  • One recent passport-size photo (white background, 33×48mm)
  • Round-trip flight bookings or travel itinerary
  • Hotel reservations for your entire stay
  • Bank statements showing sufficient funds (3 months typically)
  • Travel insurance documentation (some consulates require this)

Processing: 4–5 business days standard. Express and rush options available. Fees typically $60–160 USD (varies by nationality). Apply 4–6 weeks before departure.

Single vs. Double Entry

If you plan to visit Hong Kong or Macau and re-enter mainland China, you need a double-entry visa — a single-entry visa is consumed on first entry. Multiple-entry visas (1 or 10 years) are generally for frequent visitors with strong China travel history.

At the Border

Chinese immigration at major airports is professional and efficient. Have ready: passport (with visa if required), completed Arrival Card (on the flight or at airport), your first hotel's address, and return/onward flight details. Fingerprints and a photo are taken from all foreign visitors — standard procedure, not cause for concern.

Staying Connected in China: eSIM, Roaming & SIM Cards

Getting reliable mobile data in China is simple once you know your options. Set up your connectivity before you fly, and you'll have working maps, messaging, and payments from the moment you land.

Sort out your data plan before you board — not after you land in Shanghai.

Why Set Up Connectivity in Advance

In China, your phone is essential — it's your map, translator, payment method, and ride-hailing app all in one. Mobile payments via Alipay and WeChat Pay, navigation with Amap, and booking taxis with DiDi all need a working data connection. Sorting this out before you arrive means you step off the plane ready to go, rather than hunting for a SIM card counter with luggage in tow.

You have three solid options for mobile data. The best choice depends on your phone, your budget, and how much data you'll need.

Option 1: International eSIM (Easiest)

An eSIM from a provider like Airalo, Nomad, or Holafly installs digitally on your phone before you leave home — no physical SIM swap required. You buy a China data plan online, scan a QR code to install it, and it activates when you land. Many of these plans route through international networks, which keeps your familiar apps and services working smoothly while traveling. Typical prices: $15–35 for 5–10GB over 7–30 days.

Check that your phone supports eSIM first — most flagship phones from 2019 onward do (iPhone XS and later, recent Samsung Galaxy, Google Pixel). This is the most convenient option for most travelers.

Why eSIM Is Popular with Travelers

No need to find a store, no swapping out your home SIM, and many international eSIM plans keep your usual apps and maps working seamlessly. Set it up at home in five minutes and it's ready when you land.

Option 2: International Roaming from Your Home Carrier

Many carriers (T-Mobile, Vodafone, EE, Telstra, and others) include China in their international roaming packages. This is the simplest option — your existing number just works — but it's often the most expensive, and speeds may be throttled on basic plans. Check your carrier's China roaming rates and daily caps before you travel. Roaming plans also tend to keep your home apps and services working as they do at home.

Option 3: Local Chinese SIM Card

Available at major airports and carrier stores (China Mobile, China Unicom, China Telecom). Prices are excellent — ¥50–200 for a month with generous data — and speeds are the fastest available. You'll need your passport to register, which is standard. The trade-off: a local SIM is optimized for domestic Chinese apps and services, so pair it with the offline tools below if you rely on international apps.

Smart Backup: Download Offline Tools at Home

Whatever connectivity option you choose, download these before you travel so you're covered even with patchy signal:

  • Offline maps — Download your city maps in Maps.me, and download offline areas in Google Maps while on home Wi-Fi. Both then work without a live connection.
  • Google Translate offline pack — Download the Chinese (Simplified) language pack so the camera-translate and conversation features work anywhere, no data needed.
  • Amap (高德地图) — China's most accurate navigation app, with an English interface. Install before you go.

The Essential App Stack

Set these up before departure so they're ready when you land:

  • WeChat (微信) — messaging, payments, and how you'll communicate with hotels and guides
  • Alipay (支付宝) — mobile payments, with your foreign Visa or Mastercard linked
  • Amap (高德地图) — navigation and public transit, English available
  • DiDi (滴滴) — ride-hailing, linked to Alipay or WeChat Pay
  • Trip.com — hotel and high-speed rail bookings in English
  • Google Translate — with the offline Chinese pack downloaded

Hotel Wi-Fi

Almost all hotels provide free Wi-Fi. Major international chains (Marriott, Hilton, IHG, Hyatt) offer reliable, fast connections. Budget hotels and guesthouses vary — keep some mobile data available as a backup for important tasks like checking maps or confirming bookings.

Essential China Travel Apps — Install Before You Fly

The right apps make the difference between a smooth trip and constant friction. Here's what to install, why each matters specifically in China, and why it all has to happen before you board.

Your phone is your wallet, translator, map, and taxi in China — set it up before you land.

The Golden Rule: Set Everything Up Before You Fly

Download and configure your travel apps while you're still at home on fast Wi-Fi. Some apps are easier to find and set up in your home country's app store, and account verification (especially for payment apps) goes more smoothly before you travel.

The traveler who sets up Alipay, WeChat, maps, and a translation app at home steps off the plane fully equipped — ready to pay, navigate, and communicate from minute one. The traveler who waits until arrival spends their first day troubleshooting instead of sightseeing. Do it before you board.

Payments (Set Up First, At Home)

Alipay 支付宝 — Priority #1

China's dominant payment app. Used for street food, metro rides, tourist attractions, restaurants, and shops. Since 2023, the international version accepts foreign Visa, Mastercard, JCB, and Discover cards. Set up your account, verify with passport, and link your card before leaving home.

Real-world note: Many chain restaurants (including the popular Luckin Coffee) only accept orders through mini-programs within Alipay or WeChat — there's no counter ordering at all. Without these apps, you genuinely can't order at these places.

WeChat 微信 — Payments + Communication

WeChat is China's all-in-one app: messaging, payments, mini-programs, and social media. Your hotel, tour guide, driver, and every Chinese contact uses WeChat. WeChat Pay works with foreign cards. Create your account and link a card before departure — note that WeChat requires verification by an existing user during signup, so set this up at home.

Navigation

Amap / Gaode Maps 高德地图 — Most Accurate in China

More accurate than most Western map apps for Chinese streets, real-time transit, and business listings. Set the language to English in settings. Download before leaving home and it'll be ready the moment you land — no setup needed on arrival.

Maps.me — Offline Backup

Download Maps.me and pre-download offline maps for every city you're visiting. Works entirely without internet — invaluable when you run out of mobile data or lose signal. Has surprisingly detailed Chinese city maps.

Google Maps — With Offline Pack Downloaded

While on fast Wi-Fi at home, download offline maps for every Chinese city on your itinerary. With offline maps loaded, Google Maps works without an internet connection — handy as a familiar backup alongside Amap. For most live navigation and transit directions in China, Amap is the more reliable choice.

Transport

Trip.com — Trains + Hotels

Best English-language platform for booking China's high-speed rail and hotels. Accepts foreign credit cards. Sends e-tickets by email — show the QR code at the station gate. You'll use this throughout the trip for booking adjustments.

DiDi 滴滴 — China's Uber

Operates in virtually every Chinese city. Set up your account and link Alipay or WeChat Pay before arriving. Drivers see your destination in Chinese on their screen even if you can't communicate verbally — a huge advantage over street taxis.

Communication & Translation

Google Translate — Offline Chinese Pack

Download the Chinese (Simplified) offline pack while on home Wi-Fi. Works without internet once installed. The camera translation feature — point at a menu, sign, or label and it translates in real time — is one of the most practically useful travel tools ever created. The conversation mode (tap to speak, phone translates aloud) bridges verbal communication gaps.

Complete Checklist

✓ Alipay — passport verified, foreign card linked, metro QR tested
✓ WeChat — account verified, backup card linked
✓ Amap — set to English
✓ Maps.me — offline maps downloaded for all cities
✓ Google Translate — offline Chinese pack downloaded
✓ Google Maps — offline city maps downloaded
✓ DiDi — account created, payment linked
✓ Trip.com — account created
✓ Mobile data sorted — eSIM, roaming, or airport SIM planned

Where to Stay in China on a First Trip

Not all hotels in China can legally accept foreign guests. And even among those that do, location choices dramatically affect how much time you spend in transit versus sightseeing. Here's how to choose well in every major city.

The right neighborhood puts you within walking distance of the action — the wrong one costs 45 minutes each way, every day.

The Foreign Passport Rule

Chinese law requires hotels to be officially registered to host foreign nationals. Many budget guesthouses, B&Bs, and small hotels are not registered — they'll turn you away at check-in. Always book through Booking.com, Trip.com, or Agoda, which only show properties with foreign-guest authorization.

Airbnb Warning

Many Airbnb properties in China cannot legally host foreigners. Chinese law requires the host to register your passport details with local police within 24 hours. Officially registered hotels do this automatically. Always confirm with an Airbnb host that they can legally register a foreign passport before booking.

Where to Stay in Beijing

Best for First Timers: Dongcheng District 东城区

Dongcheng puts you walking distance from the Forbidden City, Tiananmen Square, and the historic hutong alleyways around Nanluoguxiang. Multiple subway lines connect you to everything else. Bell Tower, Drum Tower, and the Wangfujing shopping street are nearby. The best base for a first Beijing visit.

Also Good: Xicheng District 西城区

Quieter than Dongcheng. Close to Beihai Park and the Houhai lake bar district. Good selection of boutique hutong hotels — converted courtyard residences with carved wooden beams and private garden courtyard. Good metro access.

Where to Stay in Shanghai

Best: The Bund / Huangpu District

Staying near The Bund gives you direct access to Shanghai's iconic waterfront, easy metro access to Pudong's towers, and walkability to Xintiandi (upscale dining and nightlife). Options range from boutique art deco hotels to large international chains. Higher prices, but worth it for a first visit.

Also Good: Jing'an District

Central and upscale, with excellent metro connectivity. Near Jing'an Temple, some of Shanghai's best restaurants, and the Former French Concession's tree-lined streets and independent boutiques.

Where to Stay in Xi'an

Best: Inside or Near the City Walls

Stay within Xi'an's ancient walled city for atmosphere and walking access to the Muslim Quarter (回民街), Bell Tower, Drum Tower, and the city's evening market. Many hotels occupy converted heritage buildings. The Terracotta Warriors are 40 minutes east by metro — perfect as a day trip.

Where to Stay in Guilin & Yangshuo

For the Li River cruise, stay in central Guilin for easy access to the boarding points. But for the most atmospheric stay, consider at least 1–2 nights in Yangshuo (the cruise endpoint) — surrounded by dramatic karst scenery, with excellent guesthouses overlooking rice paddies, and significantly cheaper than Guilin city hotels.

Best 10-Day China Itinerary for First-Time Visitors

10 days is enough to cover China's greatest hits without exhausting yourself. The Beijing–Xi'an–Shanghai circuit is the classic first-timer's route for good reason — and a scenic add-on at the end makes it complete.

Start in Beijing — ancient, vast, and utterly unlike anywhere else on earth.

The Route at a Glance

DaysCityKey Highlights
1–3BeijingForbidden City, Great Wall (Mutianyu), Summer Palace, hutong walk
4–5Xi'anTerracotta Warriors, City Walls, Muslim Quarter night market
6–8ShanghaiThe Bund, Pudong towers, Yu Garden, Suzhou day trip
9–10Guilin + YangshuoLi River cruise, karst cycling, rice terraces

Days 1–3: Beijing

Day 1 — Arrival & Tiananmen

Check in to a Dongcheng hotel near the Forbidden City. Walk to Tiananmen Square at dusk — the atmosphere of this vast plaza at nightfall is unforgettable. Dinner at a traditional Peking duck restaurant: Da Dong (full ceremony), or Siji Minfu (excellent value, favored by locals). Stroll the hutong lanes north of the Forbidden City before bed.

Day 2 — Great Wall at Mutianyu

Full-day Great Wall trip. The Mutianyu section is 90 minutes from central Beijing, offers a spectacular restored stretch, and has fewer crowds than Badaling. Cable car up, walk 2–3 hours, toboggan back down. Book the 8:30am entry for morning light before tour groups arrive.

Day 3 — Forbidden City & Temple of Heaven

Book timed Forbidden City entry online in advance (maximum 80,000 visitors per day, sells out on weekends). Allow 3 hours minimum. Temple of Heaven in the afternoon — arrive early to watch residents doing tai chi and ballroom dancing in the surrounding park. Evening hutong bicycle tour.

Days 4–5: Xi'an (G-train, 4.5 hours, ~¥515)

Day 4 — Terracotta Warriors

The museum is 40 minutes east by metro. Allow 3–4 hours for the three pits and the bronze chariot exhibition. Go against the tourist flow: start with Pit 3 (smallest), then Pit 2, finish with Pit 1 (the most dramatic — over 6,000 figures). Return to Xi'an for dinner in the Muslim Quarter.

Day 5 — City Walls & Muslim Quarter

Rent a bicycle at the South Gate (¥50/hour) and cycle the complete 14km circuit atop Xi'an's ancient city walls — one of the few intact city-wall circuits remaining in China, with views over both ancient and modern Xi'an. Big Wild Goose Pagoda in the afternoon. Evening in the Muslim Quarter: lamb skewers, biang biang noodles, pomegranate juice, and rou jia mo (braised meat in sesame bun).

Days 6–8: Shanghai (G-train ~6 hrs, or fly ~2 hrs)

Day 6 — The Bund & Pudong

Morning Bund walk — arrive before 9am for the best light. Cross to Pudong: the Shanghai Tower observation deck (118th floor, 632m) gives a full-city panorama. Afternoon in Xintiandi's restaurants and bars. Dinner in the Former French Concession.

Day 7 — Suzhou Day Trip

Suzhou is 25 minutes from Shanghai by G-train (¥35). The UNESCO-listed classical gardens — particularly Humble Administrator's Garden (拙政园) — are genuinely beautiful. Far less crowded than Shanghai proper. Return for dinner.

Day 8 — Yu Garden & Old City

Yu Garden and the surrounding old-city bazaar in the morning (arrive before 9am). M50 Art District or Tianzifang in the afternoon for independent galleries and boutiques. Bund rooftop bar at sunset for the farewell Shanghai panorama.

Days 9–10: Guilin & Yangshuo (fly from Shanghai, ~2.5 hrs)

Day 9 — Li River Cruise

The Li River cruise from Guilin to Yangshuo (4–5 hours) is one of Asia's most famous scenic journeys. Book government tourist boats for the officially designated route. Arrive in Yangshuo for dinner and an evening on West Street.

Day 10 — Yangshuo Countryside

Morning bike ride through rice fields and karst villages (¥30–50/day rental). Moon Hill hike (1 hour) for panoramic views. Afternoon bus back to Guilin (1 hour) for onward flight.

Transport Summary

Beijing → Xi'an: G-train 4.5 hrs, ~¥515 (2nd class)
Xi'an → Shanghai: G-train ~6 hrs or fly ~2 hrs
Shanghai → Guilin: fly ~2.5 hrs, ~¥400–800
All trains bookable on Trip.com — book 30 days ahead.

What to Skip on a First Trip

Don't try to add more cities. Chengdu or Zhangjiajie can replace the Guilin add-on, but doing all three in 10 days leads to exhaustion. Save Yunnan, Tibet, and Xinjiang for a second trip — each deserves at least a week.

China in Spring

Cherry blossoms, clear skies, mild temperatures, and a country coming back to life after winter. Spring is China's most photogenic season — and still shoulder season for crowds.

March · April · May
春Spring 夏Summer 秋Autumn 冬Winter

Why Spring Stands Out

Between the bitter cold of northern winters and the humid heat of summer, spring opens a golden window across China. Most of the country settles into a comfortable 15–22°C. Beijing and Xi'an skies clear of winter haze. Hillsides in Yunnan burst into rapeseed yellow. Wuhan's cherry blossom season — 350,000 trees across one university campus — rivals Japan's famous hanami.

It's also shoulder season: the summer crowds haven't arrived, spring holidays (Qingming in early April, Labour Day in early May) are short, and hotel rates are significantly lower than July and August.

Yunnan rapeseed in bloom. Warm in the south, cool in Beijing (8–15°C). Low tourist numbers nationwide. Best value hotel rates of spring.

Cherry blossoms across central China. Perfect Great Wall hiking weather (12–20°C). Qingming (early April) brings brief domestic travel surges.

May

Peak spring warmth. Guilin's rice fields vivid green. Last comfortable window before summer heat and crowds arrive in eastern cities.

Best Spring Destinations

Beijing & The Great Wall

Spring is the single best season for the Great Wall. Mutianyu and Jinshanling are clothed in fresh green, temperatures are ideal for hiking, and summer crowds haven't arrived. Book the 8:30am slot for morning light before tour groups appear. The Forbidden City's moat is lined with weeping willows and peach blossoms in April — a scene summer visitors never see.

China in Summer

Eastern cities swelter, but China's highlands and plateaus come alive. Summer is the only viable season for Tibet — and the best time for Yunnan, Zhangjiajie, and Qinghai.

June · July · August
春Spring 夏Summer 秋Autumn 冬Winter

The Full Summer Picture

Beijing, Shanghai, Xi'an, and Chengdu in July and August are hot and humid — 35–38°C with oppressive humidity in the south. They're also at peak crowds and peak prices. For these cities, spring or autumn is significantly better.

But summer is the only viable season for Tibet. The Tibetan Plateau's altitude (Lhasa at 3,650m) keeps temperatures at 15–22°C, summer rains stay mostly east of the plateau, and roads to remote areas like Nam-tso Lake and Everest Base Camp are fully open.

June

Tibet permits processing. Yunnan lush but wet. Cities still bearable before July peak heat. Best window to secure accommodation.

July

Tibet at its finest. Eastern cities very hot. Zhangjiajie dramatic in monsoon mist. Inner Mongolia grasslands peak.

August

Qinghai Lake rapeseed bloom. Hottest month in eastern cities. Last good window for Tibet before autumn.

Best Summer Destinations

Tibet · Best June–September

Tibet — Lhasa, Yamdrok Lake & Everest Base Camp

Tibet requires a Tibet Travel Permit (TTF) plus your Chinese visa — apply through a registered travel agency at least 3–4 weeks ahead. Summer is the most reliable window. Lhasa's Potala Palace and Jokhang Temple are extraordinary. The 5-day drive to Everest Base Camp via the Friendship Highway crosses passes above 5,000m. Acclimatize 2 days in Lhasa before attempting higher destinations.

Zhangjiajie — Avatar Mountains in Monsoon Mist

Yunnan — Lijiang, Shangri-La & Tiger Leaping Gorge

Summer is the green season: Yuanyang rice terraces are vivid, wildflowers carpet the Tibetan borderlands, and Tiger Leaping Gorge is at full dramatic power. China in Autumn

Clear skies, golden foliage, comfortable temperatures, and the best light of the year for photography. Autumn is the finest season to travel China — especially for first-time visitors.

September · October · November
春Spring 夏Summer 秋Autumn 冬Winter

Why Autumn Wins

The summer humidity lifts. Beijing's sky turns its legendary deep blue. Temperatures settle into a perfect 18–26°C across most of the country. The rice paddies turn gold. Maple forests on the Great Wall's mountain sections ignite with colour. September through November is when China looks exactly like it does in the photographs.

September

Summer heat fading. Tibet still open. Guilin at its clearest. Shoulder season prices — best value of the whole year.

October

Peak autumn colour. Avoid Oct 1–7 Golden Week. Mid-to-late October is the sweet spot — foliage peaks, crowds manageable, weather perfect.

November

Quiet and crisp. Foliage at peak in northern China. Very few tourists everywhere. Cold starts in Beijing from late November.

Golden Week Warning (Oct 1–7)

China's National Day holiday is the country's largest domestic travel period. Hotels double in price, trains book out within hours of opening, and major sights become seriously crowded. Plan to arrive before September 28 or after October 8.

Best Autumn Destinations

Beijing · Peak mid-Oct to mid-Nov

Beijing — Imperial Autumn

The Forbidden City's golden rooftops against Beijing's deep autumn-blue sky is one of China's defining images. The Great Wall at Mutianyu and Jinshanling is draped in red and gold foliage from mid-October. Xiangshan (Fragrant Hills) Park turns vermillion in late October — a specific autumn pilgrimage for Beijing locals. The full Forbidden City experience without summer crowds is reason enough to visit in autumn.

China in Winter

Half the crowds, the best hotel prices of the year, snow on the Great Wall, and one of the world's most spectacular festivals in Harbin. Winter rewards travelers who don't mind the cold.

December · January · February
春Spring 夏Summer 秋Autumn 冬Winter

The Case for Winter Travel

Winter is China's secret season for international visitors. While domestic tourists mostly stay home in December and January, foreign travelers who brave the cold find hotel prices 30–50% lower, no queues at the Forbidden City, and a different, quieter China that summer crowds never experience.

A snow-covered Great Wall in January — with almost no one else on the battlements — is one of the most powerful travel experiences in China. The Forbidden City's red walls and golden rooftops against a clear winter sky have a dramatic clarity that summer haze never allows. And Harbin's Ice Festival is simply one of the world's most extraordinary spectacles.

December

Cold but manageable. Ice festival construction begins in Harbin. Quiet temples nationwide. Best value hotel prices of the year.

January

Harbin Ice Festival peak (Jan 5–Feb 5). Avoid Spring Festival travel rush (late Jan/early Feb). Northern cities -10 to -25°C.

February

Spring Festival temple fairs in Beijing are spectacular. Cities then quiet as millions travel home. Spring begins in March.

Best Winter Destinations

Harbin · Peak January–February

Harbin — Ice & Snow Festival

The Harbin International Ice and Snow Festival (January–late February) is one of the world's genuinely unmissable experiences. Ice and Snow World features palaces, castles, and sculptures carved from 250,000 cubic metres of ice cut from the frozen Songhua River — some structures reach 20 metres tall. At night, internal LED lighting turns everything iridescent: blues, greens, golds through walls of translucent ice. Temperatures drop to -25°C; dress in full thermal layers, but absolutely go.

Beijing · Best December–February

Beijing — Empty Palaces & Snow on the Wall

Beijing in winter is cold (-5 to -10°C in January) but manageable with proper layers. The Forbidden City on a clear winter morning has a dramatic clarity that summer haze never achieves. A snow-covered Great Wall at Mutianyu in January — with almost no one else there — is one of China's most memorable experiences. Hotel prices can be 40–60% below summer peak, and there are no queues at any major sight.

Browse Beijing winter tours →

Yunnan · Warm December–February

Yunnan — China's Winter Warmth

While northern China freezes, Yunnan is mild year-round. Kunming (the "Spring City") averages 14°C in January with frequent sunshine. Dali on Erhai Lake is pleasant for cycling. Lijiang's old town is beautiful with far fewer tourists in winter, and the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain backdrop is most visible on clear winter days. The tropical south — Xishuangbanna — stays warm (22°C) year-round.

Browse Yunnan winter tours →
Spring Festival Warning

Chinese New Year (Spring Festival) falls in late January or early February. Hundreds of millions of people travel home — the world's largest annual human migration. Trains book out months ahead. Prices triple. Many restaurants and shops close for a week. Plan specifically around the festival, or avoid China during this period entirely.

What to Wear in Northern China

For Beijing, Xi'an, and Harbin in winter: thermal base layers (top and bottom), a down jacket rated to at least -15°C, insulated waterproof boots, a hat covering your ears, thick gloves, and a neck gaiter. Hand warmers (¥5–10 at any convenience store) make a big difference on long outdoor days.

10 Things First-Time Visitors Wish They Knew

China rewards a little preparation. These are the practical details that make the difference between a smooth first trip and a frustrating one — the things experienced travelers always tell first-timers before they go.

01

Set up mobile payments first

China is nearly cashless. Link a foreign Visa or Mastercard to Alipay before you fly — many shops, taxis, and even street vendors no longer take cash easily. This is the single most important thing to do before departure. See our full payment guide →

02

Book high-speed trains 30 days out

Tickets open exactly 30 days ahead and sell out fast around holidays. Book early on Trip.com (English, takes foreign cards) and collect with your passport at the station. Read the rail guide →

03

Book hotels that take foreign guests

Not every hotel is licensed to host foreign passports. Book through Booking.com or Trip.com, which filter for properties that can legally check you in — and avoid being turned away on arrival. Where to stay →

04

Carry your passport everywhere

You'll need it to enter train stations, collect rail tickets, check into hotels, and buy a SIM card. Keep it on you, and store a clear photo on your phone as a backup.

05

Download a translation app

Install Google Translate with the offline Chinese pack before you go. The camera-translate feature reads menus, signs, and labels instantly — it's the closest thing to a superpower for navigating daily life. Essential apps →

06

Use DiDi instead of hailing taxis

China's ride-hailing app (like Uber) lets you input your destination in advance, so there's no language barrier with the driver. Far less stressful than flagging a cab when you don't speak Mandarin.

07

Learn three phrases

Nǐ hǎo (hello), Xièxiè (thank you), and Duōshǎo qián? (how much?). A little effort is warmly received, and the last one is genuinely useful at markets. More on language & culture →

08

Dining is shared, tipping isn't

Dishes arrive in the middle for everyone to share, not as individual plates. Pour tea for others before yourself. And don't tip — it's simply not part of the culture and can cause confusion.

09

Avoid the Golden Week crush

The first week of October and Spring Festival (Jan/Feb) are the world's biggest travel periods. Sites overflow, prices triple, trains vanish. Plan around these dates, not during them. Best time to visit →

10

Carry some cash as backup

Keep ¥500–1000 in cash for the rare vendor, temple, or rural spot where mobile payment isn't an option — and for when your phone battery runs low. A portable charger is your best friend here.

Health & Safety · 7 min read · Updated May 2026

China Health & Safety Guide for Travelers

China is one of the safest countries in the world for tourists — violent crime against visitors is rare, and you can walk most cities late at night without concern. Here's how to stay healthy, what to pack, and how to handle the rare problem if it comes up.

A little preparation means you can relax and focus on the trip.

Is China Safe for Tourists?

Yes — remarkably so. China has very low rates of violent crime, and tourists are rarely targeted. Major cities like Beijing, Shanghai, and Chengdu are safe to explore on foot at almost any hour. Extensive public-area cameras and a strong police presence mean petty street crime is also low compared with many Western tourist cities.

The main risks for travelers are ordinary ones: pickpocketing in crowded tourist spots, occasional scams targeting foreigners, traffic, and minor stomach upsets from unfamiliar food. None of these should worry you — just travel with normal awareness.

Emergency Numbers in China

120 — Ambulance / medical emergency
110 — Police
119 — Fire
Operators may have limited English. Have your hotel's name written in Chinese, and consider asking a hotel staff member to help place a call if needed.

Common Scams to Recognize

These are uncommon but worth knowing, especially around major tourist sites in Beijing and Shanghai:

  • The "tea ceremony" scam — Friendly English-speaking "students" invite you to a tea house, then present a huge bill. Politely decline invitations from strangers to visit a specific tea house or bar.
  • Art gallery invitations — Similar pattern, pressuring you to buy overpriced "student art."
  • Taxi overcharging — Use DiDi (China's ride-hailing app) instead of unmarked cabs, so the fare is fixed and tracked in advance.
  • Rickshaw fare surprises — Agree the price in writing before getting in, or skip them entirely.

Food & Water Safety

Tap water in China is not safe to drink — but this is easy to manage. Drink bottled or boiled water only (hotels provide kettles and bottled water; restaurants serve hot tea). Use bottled water even for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach.

Street food is part of the joy of China and is generally safe when you follow simple rules: eat where there's a queue of locals (high turnover means fresh food), choose items cooked hot in front of you, and ease into spicy regional cuisines gradually. Carry basic stomach medication for the first few days while your system adjusts.

If You Have Food Allergies

Have your allergy written clearly in Chinese characters to show at restaurants — translation apps can generate this. Cross-contamination awareness is lower than in some Western countries, so be specific and cautious with serious allergies, especially peanuts and shellfish.

What to Pack in a Basic Medical Kit

  • Any prescription medications in original packaging, plus a copy of the prescription
  • Stomach remedies (anti-diarrheal, rehydration salts, antacids)
  • Pain relievers and any personal cold/allergy medicine
  • Plasters/band-aids and basic first aid
  • Hand sanitizer and travel tissues (public restrooms often lack both)
  • A surgical or N95 mask — useful on high-pollution days and in crowds

Pharmacies & Hospitals

Pharmacies (药店, yàodiàn) are widespread and stock both Western and traditional Chinese medicine. Staff rarely speak English, so use a translation app to show what you need. For anything more serious, major cities have international hospitals and VIP/international wards at top public hospitals, where English-speaking staff and Western-trained doctors are available. These cost more — which is exactly why travel insurance matters.

Travel Insurance Is Essential

Buy travel insurance with solid medical coverage and emergency evacuation before your trip. Quality care at international hospitals is excellent but expensive for non-residents, and evacuation coverage matters if you travel to remote areas like Tibet or rural Yunnan. This is the one thing you should never skip.

Air Quality

Air pollution varies by city and season — generally worse in winter and in northern industrial cities. Check an air quality index app, and on high-pollution days, an N95 mask makes outdoor sightseeing more comfortable. For most travelers on a typical 1–2 week trip, this is a minor consideration rather than a health risk.

Solo & Female Travelers

China is widely considered very safe for solo travelers, including women, who routinely report feeling comfortable traveling alone here. Normal precautions apply: stay aware in crowds, use registered transport like DiDi at night, and keep your accommodation address written in Chinese. The biggest challenge is usually the language barrier, not safety.

China Language & Culture Guide for Travelers

You don't need to speak Mandarin to have a wonderful trip to China — but a handful of phrases and a little cultural awareness go a remarkably long way. Locals genuinely appreciate the effort, and it opens doors that stay closed to other tourists.

A little cultural fluency turns a good trip into a memorable one.

The Language Situation

Mandarin Chinese (普通话, Pǔtōnghuà) is the official language, written in simplified Chinese characters. English is limited outside major hotels, airports, and top tourist sites — most taxi drivers, restaurant staff, and shopkeepers speak little or none. This sounds daunting but is very manageable with the right tools and a friendly attitude.

Your most powerful tool is a translation app. Google Translate (with the offline Chinese pack downloaded) and Pleco are both excellent. The camera-translate feature reads menus and signs instantly, and conversation mode lets you and a local speak turn-by-turn.

The Pointing Trick

Don't stress about tones. Pointing at items, showing a translated phrase on your phone, and a warm smile will get you through almost any situation. Chinese people are generally very patient and helpful with tourists who make an effort.

Dining & Table Etiquette

Meals are central to Chinese culture and one of the great joys of visiting. A few customs to know:

  • Dishes are shared. Food is ordered for the whole table and placed in the center — you take from communal plates, often with a serving spoon or the back end of your chopsticks.
  • Pour tea for others first. Filling others' cups before your own is a sign of respect. A light tap of two fingers on the table is a silent "thank you" when someone pours for you.
  • Don't stick chopsticks upright in rice. It resembles incense at a funeral and is considered unlucky. Rest them on the chopstick holder or across your bowl.
  • Trying everything is polite. Hosts show generosity by ordering abundantly; sampling a bit of each dish is appreciated.
  • Tipping is not expected — not in restaurants, taxis, or hotels. It can even cause confusion.

Face, Manners & Social Customs

The concept of "face" (面子, miànzi) — social dignity and respect — underlies many interactions. In practice, this means a few simple habits go a long way:

  • Stay calm and avoid public anger. Losing your temper or causing someone to "lose face" in public rarely helps and can make a situation worse. A patient, friendly approach gets better results.
  • Use both hands when giving or receiving something important — a business card, a gift, money, or your passport. It signals respect.
  • Accept gifts and offers graciously. A polite initial refusal before accepting is part of the social rhythm.
  • Modest dress is appreciated at temples and religious sites — cover shoulders and knees.

Temple & Site Etiquette

When visiting temples, monasteries, and religious sites: dress modestly, speak quietly, and always ask before photographing people in prayer or monks. Some halls prohibit photography entirely — look for signs or follow the lead of locals. Step over thresholds rather than on them, and walk around Buddhist sites clockwise.

Photography Courtesy

Always ask before photographing people, especially elderly people, monks, or ethnic-minority individuals in traditional dress. A smile and a gesture toward your camera is usually enough to ask permission. Some military, government, and certain religious sites prohibit photos entirely.

Regional Diversity

China is vast and culturally varied. Cantonese is widely spoken in the south (Guangdong, around Hong Kong), and many regions have their own dialects, cuisines, and customs. The 56 officially recognized ethnic groups — especially vibrant in Yunnan, Guizhou, and the western regions — add enormous cultural richness. Approaching each region with curiosity and openness is the heart of great travel in China.
